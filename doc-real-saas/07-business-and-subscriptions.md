# Business, Credits, and Offer Hypotheses

All pricing in this document is a test design, not a final price list. Currency and tax handling depend on the first market.

## Commercial thesis

Owners obtain credits and use them to create and manage a trusted company attendance group. The paid value is not merely the clock button: it is company creation, invitations, member administration, per-device approval, permission control, attendance oversight, and reliable records.

## Who pays

- The person who creates the company becomes its initial owner and funds the relevant credit account.
- Invited members sign in with personal Google accounts and join without buying individual subscriptions.
- A person may be an unpaid member of one company and a paying owner of another.
- If ownership changes, credit control and future payment responsibility must be transferred deliberately without silently transferring personal purchases.

## Credit-first commercial model

- All paid product behavior uses credits.
- A subscription periodically grants credits.
- A one-time purchase grants a fixed credit pack.
- A fixed-price capability consumes a disclosed fixed number of credits.
- Trials, referrals, compensation, and promotions can grant credits with clear labels and terms.
- Future premium capabilities can be introduced by defining a credit cost instead of inventing a separate payment model.
- Real-money price, currency, taxes, and payment receipt remain visible even though product usage is expressed in credits.

## Recommended packaging experiment

Keep packaging understandable in one glance:

- **Trial grant:** promotional credits sufficient to evaluate one company for a defined period or usage level.
- **Credit pack:** one-time purchase of a fixed number of credits.
- **Starter subscription:** recurring payment that grants a defined credit amount suitable for a small company.
- **Team subscription:** recurring payment that grants more credits and may offer a better effective rate for companies approaching 20 people.

Company creation, time-based management, active-member allowance, or optional capabilities can each have credit costs. The first pricing experiment should stay simple enough that an owner can predict the next month’s usage.

## Avoid at first

- A permanently free multi-user tier that creates support cost without a conversion path
- Charging for archived members or pending invites
- Charging invited employees a separate fee simply to join a paid company
- Feature matrices with many artificial restrictions
- Annual-only commitments before trust is established
- Usage metrics customers cannot independently understand
- A credit conversion or expiry rule hidden in fine print
- Different credit types that look identical but behave differently

## Credit and access rules

- The product shows one consistent credit balance, offer, and active-member count.
- Repeated or delayed payment updates never create duplicate charges or contradictory access.
- Payment failure does not add new paid credits and receives a communicated recovery path.
- Read/export access should remain available during a reasonable restricted state.
- Subscription cancellation stops future recurring credit grants; the treatment of unused credits must be disclosed.
- An owner can see exactly which memberships count toward the limit.
- A member cannot create a new company without enough credits or an eligible credit grant.

## Billing states to design

Trial, active subscription, one-time purchase, payment problem, restricted, canceled, and refunded are useful customer-facing concepts. Separately, credits may be available, reserved, consumed, reversed, or expired. Their language and consequences must be understandable without knowledge of a payment service.

## Validation interviews

Interview 10–15 owners/operators before locking scope. Ask about the last time attendance caused work or conflict, current process, monthly effort, who corrects records, device context, reports needed, and what they already pay. Avoid leading with the proposed solution or price.

## Pricing test

After problem interviews, show a clickable workflow and ask for a concrete next step: paid pilot, refundable deposit, or scheduled onboarding. “Would you pay?” is weak evidence; an actual commitment is strong evidence.

## Unit economics to track

- Monthly recurring revenue
- Trial-to-paid conversion
- Revenue and support time per organization
- Payment and service-delivery cost
- Gross margin
- Logo and revenue churn
- Customer acquisition cost and payback period, once acquisition becomes repeatable

## Support model

Start with in-product help, a short setup checklist, clear error messages, and email support. Capture every support request by category; recurring questions should improve the product or documentation before hiring around them.

## Go-to-market hypothesis

Begin with one narrow segment and geography where the team can personally onboard customers. Founder-led onboarding provides the fastest feedback on policy, trust, pricing, and missing workflows.
